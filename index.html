The Turnaround

From command-and-control to a shared vision

For nearly 40 years, Formula 1 was run under the tight, opaque control of a single dealmaker. When Liberty Media acquired the sport in 2016, Chase Carey, Sean Bratches, and Ross Brawn inherited a profitable business with almost no infrastructure behind it — and a clear leadership question: how do you change a culture that had stood still for decades?

“The organization wasn’t doing the things you needed to do to compete… It was run for short-term cash flow.”

— Chase Carey, CEO, Formula 1
The Old Model

One person, all the decisions

  • Chaotic, opaque decision-making
  • Fractious relationships, little trust
  • No marketing, research, or digital function
  • Dismissed younger fans and new ideas
The New Model

A leadership team with a mandate

  • Clear roles: commercial, brand, motorsport
  • Transparency and feedback with the teams
  • Formal functions built from scratch
  • Evidence-based decisions, freedom to experiment
Building the Team

Hire fresh thinking — but keep the memory of the house

The new leaders brought in outsiders from media, tech, and even rugby to build functions that never existed. But they deliberately retained long-serving staff who held institutional knowledge and key relationships. New units were given freedom to experiment. Internally, they called themselves a “40-year-old startup.”

Before hiring, the trio aligned everyone behind five shared pillars — a simple, common language that let independent teams pull in the same direction.

Teamwork Under Pressure

Two seconds. Twenty people. One flawless act.

Nowhere is teamwork more visible than the pit stop — a rehearsed, wordless choreography where a single hesitation costs a race. Behind every driver sit up to 60 trackside engineers and technicians, plus dozens more at the factory, all acting on the same live data in real time.

Healthy Competition

Rivalry that raises everyone

Great leaders don’t remove competition — they design it to be fair. For four seasons the three richest teams dominated, and true wheel-to-wheel racing grew rare. So Formula 1, the FIA, and the teams negotiated a $175m budget cap and new car rules to level the field.

The insight was cultural, not just technical: as Bratches put it, “competition elevates engagement.” A stronger contest makes every stakeholder — teams, sponsors, broadcasters, fans — better off.

Engineering as Collaboration

Marginal gains, made together

A front wing is a thousand small decisions balancing downforce against drag — the product of designers, aerodynamicists, and engineers iterating as one unit. In F1, no individual wins alone; the car is a team’s collective intelligence made physical.

Every season the car is rebuilt from scratch. That relentless cycle only works when knowledge flows freely across functions rather than pooling in silos.

Heritage & Identity

Where it began — and why culture endures

The first World Championship race ran at Silverstone in 1950. Honoring that heritage while daring to modernize is the balancing act every long-standing organization faces.

Strategy & Trust

Split-second calls, built on shared data

Through the tight streets of Monaco, a driver trusts a strategy team they cannot see. When to pit, which tyres, whether to undercut — decisions made by analysts and executed by the driver. It works only when both sides trust the same information and each other’s judgment.

This is distributed leadership: authority flows to whoever holds the best information at that moment — the essence of a high-performing team.

The Takeaways

Five leadership & teamwork lessons

Set a shared vision before you hire

The five pillars gave every new team member a common direction — alignment first, autonomy second.

Blend fresh talent with institutional memory

Outsiders brought new thinking; retained insiders kept the relationships and knowledge that made change possible.

Replace control with trust and transparency

Moving from a one-person empire to open, evidence-based decisions unlocked initiative across the organization.

Great teamwork is rehearsed, not improvised

The two-second pit stop is proof: elite teams turn preparation and shared data into flawless execution under pressure.

Design competition to lift the whole system

A fair contest — budget caps, better rules — serves teams, partners, and fans alike. Competition elevates engagement.

The Inflection Point

“If everything worked exactly as planned, you’re not doing enough.”

Carey’s restlessness captures the mindset of adaptive leadership — always one lap ahead of complacency.
